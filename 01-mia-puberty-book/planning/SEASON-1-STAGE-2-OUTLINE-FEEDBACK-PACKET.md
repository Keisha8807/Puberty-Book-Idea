## 1. PURPOSE OF THIS STAGE

This is a **Stage 2 outline review**, not a manuscript critique and not a final medical sign-off.

The goal is to test the approved Season 1 architecture before Season 2 inherits it. Reviewers are being asked whether the season earns its reader and caregiver promises through story, sequence, emotional safety, cultural specificity, educational clarity, and child dignity.

### This stage should answer

1. Does the 11-episode season work as one cumulative story rather than a topic checklist?
2. Does Mia's investigation arc earn the Episode 11 first-period payoff?
3. Will Black girls ages approximately 9–11 feel centered, respected, entertained, and supported?
4. Can a caregiver trust the approach enough to buy, share, and discuss the book?
5. Are there outline-level health, shame, privacy, stereotype, or developmental risks that must be corrected before Season 2 planning?
6. Which concerns are true architecture findings, and which belong later in episode execution?

### This stage does not authorize

- automatic changes to canon;
- episode prose drafting;
- a full-song or CROWN-origin reveal;
- replacement of the Episode 17 placeholder;
- publication, outreach, spending, or reviewer contact;
- a claim that the book has completed medical review; or
- collection of children's identities, emails, health histories, or private puberty experiences.

Reviewer findings are **evidence and recommendations**. Keisha remains the author and final decision-maker.

## 3. FIXED REVIEW TARGETS

Reviewers should test whether the outline **earns** these promises. They should not invent a different audience or dilute the Black-girl-centered mission.

### Reader

Black girls approximately ages 9–11 approaching or beginning puberty—especially girls who are curious, embarrassed, worried they are early or late, or reluctant to ask a grown-up.

### Reader transformation

> “Something weird and scary is happening, and growing up might erase who I am.”

To:

> “Puberty happens on different schedules. I can understand it, prepare for it, ask for help, and still be fully myself.”

### Parent/caregiver trust promise

> A funny, culturally affirming story that helps a Black girl begin puberty conversations with practical language, trusted-adult pathways, and guidance that will be sourced and medically reviewed before publication—without shame, fear tactics, or treating one timeline as universal.

### Story promise

- Story before lesson
- Child dignity first
- Mia acts; adults support but do not conveniently solve her central emotional problem
- Comedy without humiliation
- Black girls centered unapologetically
- Multiple trustworthy relationships rather than one all-purpose expert
- Facts flagged for credible sourcing and qualified human review before publication

## 5. REVIEWER PANEL — LEAN RESPONSIBLE VERSION

Run reviews independently before reviewers see one another's comments. This reduces groupthink and makes agreement more meaningful.

### Group 1 — Caregiver / buyer trust

**Best fit:** A Black parent or caregiver of a girl in or near the target age range; a second caregiver perspective may be added if available.

**Primary question:**

> What would make you trust this book enough to buy it, share it with a girl you care for, and use it to begin a conversation—and what would stop you?

**Focus:** buyer promise, emotional safety, adult pathways, first-period timing caveat, shame/privacy risks, and whether comedy supports rather than trivializes.

### Group 2 — Black-girl reader and dignity lens

**Best fit:** A Black children's-literature reader advocate with strong tween knowledge. Optional direct tween feedback must be privately facilitated by a trusted adult and returned without names, contact information, health history, or identifying quotes.

**Focus:** age fit, Mia's agency and voice, cultural specificity, relatability, embarrassment load, Solani's privacy, Kayla/Sophie continuity, and whether any girl becomes a lesson prop.

**Child-safety rule:** No open link, public profile, DM, child email collection, AI chat, or unmoderated submission may be used.

### Group 3 — Children's story architecture

**Best fit:** A children's acquisitions editor, developmental editor, librarian with strong story craft, or comparable middle-grade/tween story professional.

**Focus:** cumulative arc, episode order, escalation, repetition, setup/payoff, rhythm variation, finale load, stingers, and story-before-lesson execution risk.

### Group 4 — Health / medical safety

**Best fit:** A qualified pediatric clinician, adolescent-health clinician, school nurse, or health educator reviewing within their competence.

**Focus:** outline-level framing of puberty, tenderness, emotions, body image, boundaries, hygiene, menstruation, period products, acne, variation, help-seeking, and any missing red-flag language.

**Important:** This is an early risk screen only. Final factual language still requires source citations and qualified human review at manuscript stage. Record the reviewer's role and the exact version reviewed; do not label anyone a medical reviewer without consent.

### Group 5 — Educator / librarian practicality

**Best fit:** An upper-elementary educator, school librarian, counselor, or school health professional.

**Focus:** age/school realism, classroom and library trust, school-adult pathways, likely points of confusion, read-aloud sensitivity, and whether the outline creates unnecessary school-use barriers.

### Minimum responsible panel

Aim for one independent response from each group. One adult may cover two nonmedical lenses if their competence is clear. The health lane must not be replaced by AI or by an unqualified general reader.

## 6. INSTRUCTIONS TO EVERY REVIEWER

1. Review the source independently.
2. Comment on architecture, not line-level prose.
3. Cite the section or episode for every concern.
4. Separate what is observed from what is inferred.
5. Explain the likely reader/buyer impact.
6. Recommend the **smallest responsible correction**, not a wholesale rewrite.
7. Do not add generic inclusivity language that moves Black girls out of the center.
8. Do not ask for a child's personal puberty story.
9. Do not invent medical facts. Mark what requires verification.
10. Do not treat a personal preference as a safety or structural finding.

### Evidence labels

Use one label for each finding:

- **Observed** — directly present in the Flow Grid
- **Inferred** — a reasoned interpretation grounded in observed evidence
- **External** — supported by a separate, dated credible source or professional standard
- **Unavailable** — cannot be determined at outline level

### Severity labels

- **RED — Blocker:** credible medical/safety harm, child-dignity failure, major canon contradiction, or broken season architecture
- **YELLOW — Architecture decision:** meaningful concern that may require a Flow Grid or canon choice before Season 2
- **BLUE — Execution note:** important for a later episode packet or prose pass but does not require changing Level 1 architecture now
- **GRAY — Preference:** taste-based suggestion with no demonstrated promise, safety, or continuity impact

## 8. ROLE-SPECIFIC QUESTIONS

### Group 1 — Caregiver / buyer

1. Does the parent/caregiver trust promise match what the outline actually delivers?
2. What specific element most increases purchase trust?
3. What specific element most threatens trust?
4. Would you expect this book to help begin a conversation without replacing the caregiver?
5. Are the school, family, and clinical help pathways practical and reassuring?
6. Would Episode 8's accidental stain clue feel respectful under its guardrails?
7. Does Episode 11 reassure clearly enough that a period is a milestone, not instant adulthood?

### Group 2 — Black-girl reader and dignity

1. Does Mia feel like a full Black girl protagonist rather than a vehicle for lessons?
2. Does the world center Black girls naturally rather than through generic or performative language?
3. Are Mia's fears recognizable without making puberty seem monstrous?
4. Does Solani retain ownership of her room, notebook, music, period, and feelings?
5. Are Jada, Emma, Kayla, and Sophie distinct people rather than functions?
6. Where might a target reader laugh, lean in, skim, or shut down?
7. Is any episode carrying too much embarrassment at once?

### Group 3 — Story architecture

1. Does the progression feel inevitable: define → experience variation → emotions/image/boundaries/hygiene/friendship → understand periods → prepare → experience?
2. Are Episodes 8–11 paced effectively despite Episode 10 interrupting the period sequence?
3. Do Episodes 8 and 11 bend the recurring case rhythm enough?
4. Is Brenda's six-episode presence dramatically varied?
5. Does Episode 11 pay off the individual-timing, emotion, boundary, and kit setups without becoming overloaded?
6. Are the stingers creating momentum rather than making episodes feel mechanically serialized?
7. Does the three-clue music thread work as atmosphere rather than a dangling plot promise?

### Group 4 — Health / medical safety

For each flagged item, identify the episode, the risk, the level of urgency, and the kind of source or specialist needed.

1. Is any educational promise inaccurate, oversimplified in a harmful way, or developmentally mismatched?
2. Does the outline distinguish normal variation from concerns that should be shared with a trusted adult or clinician?
3. Are menstruation, body odor, chest tenderness, emotional changes, acne, and product choice framed without shame?
4. Does any episode imply that hormones excuse harmful behavior?
5. Is the outline careful enough around pain, bleeding, irritation, severe symptoms, and individual differences?
6. Are there areas where the outline promises more than one episode can responsibly teach?
7. Which factual claims will require line-level sources and final human review?

### Group 5 — Educator / librarian

1. Do Helderberg's school settings and staff roles feel plausible for a fifth grader?
2. Are the school-adult pathways clear without making school staff replace family or clinical care?
3. Which episode would be easiest and hardest to recommend in a school/library context, and why?
4. Does any scene create avoidable privacy, policy, or classroom-use concerns?
5. Are the friendship, boundary, body-image, and health topics separated clearly enough?
6. What caregiver-facing support would make school/library use more trustworthy later?

## 10. INTERNAL SYNTHESIS RULES

The Workhorse may compile feedback; it may not approve changes.

### Synthesis order

1. Preserve each reviewer's independent response.
2. Remove personal contact information and unnecessary identifying details from the working synthesis.
3. Group duplicate findings without erasing disagreement.
4. Separate RED, YELLOW, BLUE, and GRAY findings.
5. Identify which observations are shared across two or more review lanes.
6. Check every proposed change against Active Canon and the five locked AQ-009 decisions.
7. Route only true RED/YELLOW decisions to Keisha.
8. Carry BLUE findings into the correct future Book Factory packet.
9. Archive GRAY preferences unless Keisha specifically wants to consider them.

### No automatic majority rule

Three preferences do not outweigh one credible medical or child-safety concern. One unsupported concern does not automatically overturn approved architecture. Weight evidence, competence, impact, and source quality—not vote count.
