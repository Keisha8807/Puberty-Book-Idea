## **Clinician Practice Perspectives**

**Dr. Lauren Mitchell — Clinician focused on simple, child-friendly explanations**

Breast buds can feel tender, and one side may begin developing before the other. That is generally a normal part of puberty. Mia should tell an adult if the pain is severe, does not improve, follows an injury, or occurs with redness, warmth, fever, unusual discharge, or another change that worries her.

The story does not need to list every possibility. Something like, “Tender can be normal, but strong pain or anything that scares you deserves an adult’s attention,” may be enough for the child-facing scene. Exact language should still be reviewed.

I would explain menstruation as the body releasing blood and tissue from inside the uterus through the vagina. It happens as part of a menstrual cycle and is not an injury.

For this age, I would avoid a long explanation of hormones during the first conversation. A simple anatomical explanation, followed by Mia’s practical questions, should be sufficient. More detail can go in back matter.

The most important warning signs are:

Bleeding so heavy that a pad becomes soaked very quickly for several hours.

Bleeding that continues longer than seven days.

Severe pain, fainting, marked weakness, or looking unusually pale.

Symptoms that prevent ordinary activities or make the child feel seriously unwell.

The story should direct the child to tell a trusted adult rather than expect her to decide whether the situation is medically urgent by herself.

Deodorant helps control odor. Antiperspirant reduces sweating, and many products contain both. Fragrance adds scent but does not wash the body or necessarily prevent sweat or odor.

That distinction can be made in one short exchange. The episode does not need to debate ingredients or recommend a particular product.

Explain that acne is common during puberty and is not proof that someone is dirty. Picking, scrubbing hard, or using harsh internet hacks may irritate the skin and contribute to marks that remain after the pimple improves.

The story can recommend telling a caregiver and using gentle care without naming treatments. Persistent, painful, scarring, or emotionally upsetting acne should be discussed with a health professional.

Yes, if every emotional change is explained as hormones. Puberty can influence emotional intensity, but relationships, stress, sleep, school, personality, and ordinary life also matter. Solani crying at a commercial should not become medical evidence that her hormones are controlling her.

The outline already points in the right direction by saying hormones are one influence rather than an excuse or complete explanation.

A simple first-period kit might include pads, clean underwear, a small disposal bag, tissues or wipes intended for ordinary external cleanup, and a discreet pouch. A change of clothing may be useful if it fits realistically in Mia’s backpack.

The most important “item” is a plan: know which trusted adult to approach and where school supplies are available. Avoid adding medication unless it is handled by a caregiver or clinician and reviewed separately.

I would say, “Many people can continue school, sports, and other normal activities during their periods, but you do not have to ignore significant pain or heavy bleeding.” That supports ordinary activity without suggesting that every child must push through discomfort.

**Dr. Priya Shah — Clinician focused on avoiding accidental medical advice**

The normal pattern described in Episode 2 is broadly sound: breast development may be uneven, and early breast buds can be sore or tender. The concern is that “normal” can sound dismissive.

The adult should acknowledge Mia’s pain, ask whether anything else is happening, and give her a clear way to return for help. The scene should not imply that all breast pain in a child is automatically puberty.

Start with the body part and process, not a metaphor that could confuse the reader. Menstruation involves blood and tissue leaving the uterus through the vagina. It is not a cut, injury, punishment, or evidence that a girl has become an adult.

The language can be brief, but using accurate anatomical words is preferable to phrases such as “down there.”

I would prioritize heavy or prolonged bleeding, severe or worsening pain, fainting or significant weakness, and any symptom that makes the child look or feel seriously ill. The precise thresholds belong in medically reviewed back matter rather than Mia’s dialogue.

A fictional ten-year-old should not be responsible for monitoring medical criteria alone. Her instruction should be to tell a trusted adult when something feels severe, frightening, or difficult to manage.

Washing removes sweat, oil, and odor-causing bacteria. Deodorant addresses odor. Antiperspirant reduces sweat. Fragrance only adds or masks scent.

Do not frame one product choice as the universally correct choice. Children with sensitive skin or skin conditions may need individualized guidance.

Keep the story at the level of safe principles: acne is common, dirt is not the sole cause, harsh scrubbing and picking can worsen irritation, and darker marks may remain after inflammation—particularly in deeper skin tones.

Do not provide a homemade recipe or describe the unsafe hacks in enough detail to make them tempting. Avoid recommending active ingredients, strengths, or treatment schedules unless a pediatric clinician or dermatologist reviews them.

Yes. A “feelings forecast” could accidentally teach children that puberty makes girls irrational or unreliable. The story should separate the validity of feelings from responsibility for actions while avoiding the idea that every reaction comes from hormones.

It would help if Mia’s own emotional reaction has an understandable non-hormonal cause, such as disappointment or stress.

Pads are reasonable for Mia’s basic school kit because they can be shown simply. Clean underwear, a small bag for soiled clothing, and contact information or a plan for reaching an adult are also appropriate.

The story can acknowledge that different products exist without turning the episode into a product guide. Medication should remain outside the child-packed kit unless caregiver and school procedures are addressed.

Use flexible language: “A period usually does not mean you have to stop ordinary activities. Some people choose to rest or adjust what they are doing, and strong pain or very heavy bleeding should be shared with an adult.”

Avoid making continued participation sound like a test of toughness.

**Dr. Renee Coleman — Clinician focused on story content versus back matter**

The story should encourage adult help when acne is painful, persistent, leaving scars or marks, or making Mia feel distressed.

I would show Mia returning to an ordinary activity after receiving help, while making clear that this is her choice and her symptoms are manageable. Another character can mention that resting or seeking help is also valid.

## **Clinical Safety Screens**

**Dr. Maya Patel — Clinical screen: generally reassuring**

**1\.** At the outline level, I do not see an obvious medical premise that would make the story unsafe. Breast tenderness and uneven development can occur normally during puberty. Body odor and acne are also common changes, and menstruation is correctly treated as normal bleeding rather than an injury.

I would want to review the actual explanation of menstruation before approving it. “Uterine bleeding” may be accurate in a broad sense, but a child-friendly explanation should make clear that the blood and tissue leave the uterus through the vagina. I would also review every acne “hack” and scented-product experiment before publication so a child cannot mistake an unsafe idea for something worth trying.

**2\.** The outline repeatedly tells Mia to bring pain or concerns to a trusted adult or clinician, which is good. It does not yet define what kinds of symptoms need that attention. “Pain and concerns” is too broad to be very useful by itself.

The final text should give a few understandable examples, such as bleeding that is extremely heavy or lasts longer than expected, severe pain, fainting or unusual weakness, a painful or worsening breast problem, or acne that is painful, severe, scarring, or affecting the child emotionally. The wording should be reviewed so it informs without alarming the reader.

**3\.** The overall framing is non-shaming. Body odor is separated from being dirty, acne is not blamed on poor hygiene, and menstruation is not treated as disgusting or as instant womanhood. I especially like the rule against investigating who smells.

Be careful with jokes involving stained clothing, odor, and pimples. The situation can be funny, but the child experiencing the body change should never become the punch line.

**4\.** The book should explain what Mia practically does when her period starts—not only that she uses the kit. A reader may need to know how to place and change a pad, what to do with a used product, that early periods may be irregular, and whom to ask for supplies at school.

I would also include a simple reminder that bodies do not follow one exact schedule. Mia being ten is plausible, but readers should not conclude that ten is when their period is supposed to begin.

**Dr. Elena Rodriguez — Clinical screen: cautious about missing thresholds**

**1\.** Nothing is clearly inaccurate, but this outline contains promises rather than the actual medical language. I could not clear it for accuracy without seeing the claim sheet and relevant passages.

The mood episode needs care. Hormonal changes may influence emotions, but puberty should not become the explanation for every strong feeling or change in behavior. The story already says hormones are only one influence, which is the safer direction.

The deodorant-versus-antiperspirant explanation also needs exact wording. Deodorant primarily addresses odor, while antiperspirant reduces sweating. Fragrance by itself is not the same as either one.

**2\.** Not clearly enough yet. The outline says concerns belong with adults or clinicians, but it does not consistently show the difference between “common and uncomfortable” and “needs evaluation.” That distinction should be built into the claim sheet rather than improvised during drafting.

For menstruation, examples worth reviewing include very heavy bleeding, bleeding lasting more than seven days, severe pain, fainting, marked weakness, or symptoms that keep the child from ordinary activities. For breast development, tenderness and one side beginning first can be normal, but significant redness, fever, discharge, injury, or severe or persistent pain should not simply be dismissed as puberty.

**3\.** Mostly yes. I would reconsider language such as “smell emergency,” “structural damage,” and “crimson mystery” once it appears in prose. Those phrases may work as Mia’s exaggerated misunderstanding, but a trusted adult should respond calmly and quickly enough that the book does not accidentally amplify fear.

Also avoid suggesting that good hygiene can always eliminate body odor or acne. Children can follow a reasonable routine and still experience both.

**4\.** I would add the possibility of normal vaginal discharge before a first period, since many girls notice it and may worry that something is wrong. I would also show that the first period may be light, brownish, or different from the bright-red emergency Mia imagines.

The period-kit episode should avoid suggesting that every reader needs the same products. Pads may be the easiest product to demonstrate in this story, but the language should leave room for family preference, comfort, access, and different needs.

**Dr. Kiara Williams — Clinical screen: manuscript-review priorities**

**1\.** The broad concepts look reasonable. My main concern would be execution. The “bad hacks” in the acne episode and the fragrance experiment in the body-odor episode should not be described so specifically that children can copy them. If an irritating product is used, the story needs a safe response and should not turn that reaction into a home-treatment lesson.

I would also avoid naming medications, active skin-care ingredients, or doses unless those details are specifically reviewed. The story can safely emphasize gentle care, not picking, and asking a caregiver or clinician for help.

**2\.** The normal-versus-concerning distinction is present as an intention but not yet as usable guidance. Each health episode should probably contain one brief “tell an adult” pathway. It does not need to become a warning list, but the child should understand that persistent, severe, rapidly worsening, or frightening symptoms deserve help.

Episode 11 says urgent or concerning symptoms require support, but Mia’s actual first period appears uncomplicated. That is appropriate. Do not add a serious symptom merely to teach an emergency response unless a clinician reviews the entire plot beat.

**3\.** The framing is thoughtful and mostly free of shame. The strongest choices are that menstruation is not adulthood, acne is not dirtiness, hygiene is care rather than moral worth, and girls are allowed privacy.

I would watch the adult dialogue. Statements like “this is normal” can feel dismissive if Mia is hurting or frightened. Something closer to “This can happen during puberty, and I still want to hear what you’re feeling” would validate her without assuming every symptom is harmless.

**4\.** A girl this age may need more information about what happens immediately after discovering blood: check where the blood is coming from, tell a trusted adult, use a pad or other available product, change soiled clothing if possible, and ask the school nurse for help.

She may also need reassurance that there is no single correct emotional response. She can feel scared, annoyed, calm, proud, embarrassed, or several things at once. That is developmental guidance rather than a medical claim, but it matters to the safety and dignity of the scene.
